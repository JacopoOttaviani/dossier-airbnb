Airbnb and Europe's Rent Crisis

In 2024, travellers spent a record 854 million nights in platform rentals across Europe. In the continent's great cities, entire streets are now filled with people sleeping in someone else's bed. Six cities, three questions: how much housing has moved onto Airbnb? What has been the impact on rents? And where crackdowns have been introduced, have they actually worked?

August 2026 · Data: Inside Airbnb, Eurostat, national statistics · ~9 min read

Photo: Barcelona's Eixample — Logan Armstrong / Unsplash

In almost every European city, you can spot small metal boxes bolted to drainpipes. Inside each is a key; behind the key, an apartment that no one calls home anymore.

That is one of the most visible signs of Airbnb's transformation of the city. Founded in 2008, the platform has grown at extraordinary speed, reshaping urban neighbourhoods and leaving a lasting mark on the long-term rental market.

This is no longer a niche phenomenon of the sharing economy. According to Eurostat, guests booked 854 million nights through Airbnb, Booking, Expedia and TripAdvisor across the EU in 2024 — a 19 per cent increase in a single year, bringing the total close to a billion when the following season is included. Paris alone recorded more platform nights than some entire EU member states. In central Rome, apartment buildings increasingly have a second, invisible population: visitors arriving with a code for the door and leaving a few days later. In Lisbon's Santa Maria Maior, the medieval parish that gave the city its name — and its fado — short-term lets outnumber the residents left on some streets.

The cities have implemented some limitations. Rome has banned the key boxes; Paris has capped the number of nights; Barcelona has pledged to abolish the trade altogether. Yet in June 2026, the six cities examined in this dossier — Rome, Barcelona, Paris, Berlin, Lisbon and London — still accounted for more than a quarter of a million active Airbnb listings between them.

So does Airbnb push up long-term rents? The evidence is clear: yes, measurably — although it is not the whole story. One of the most rigorous European studies, in Barcelona, found that Airbnb activity increased rents by 1.9 per cent across the city, and by 7 per cent in neighbourhoods where listings are concentrated. In Berlin, researchers at DIW found that every additional commercial Airbnb listing displaces between 0.23 and 0.37 homes from the long-term rental market.

These effects may not look dramatic in isolation. But they land on top of a decade in which rents across these six cities have risen by between 18 and 100 per cent. And Airbnb's impact is concentrated precisely where housing is scarcest and most sought-after: the historic centre, the walkable neighbourhood, the place where people actually want to live.

Airbnb is not the sole cause of Europe's housing crisis. But the evidence shows that it has made the shortage worse — and pushed rents higher where the pressure is already greatest.

So the cities fought back, each with its own weapon. Berlin banned unpermitted entire-home short lets as early as 2016. Paris built Europe's most sophisticated registration system and cut its annual cap to 90 nights. Lisbon froze new licences; Barcelona plans to let all 10,000 of its licences expire in 2028. London has had a 90-night limit for a decade — but almost no effective means of enforcing it. Rome, until last year, had barely any rules at all.

This dossier measures what happened next. The verdict, visible in the data, is clear: regulation can genuinely shrink the short-let market — Berlin is the proof. But nowhere has regulation, on its own, brought rents down.

01 · The map

Europe's Airbnb Pressure Points

Airbnb pressure is far from evenly distributed. Port and heritage cities — Porto, Florence, Venice, and Lisbon — bear three to four times the per-capita load of northern capitals. Paris, meanwhile, manages to be both: a major metropolis with the intensity of a resort town.

Rome's market is remarkably concentrated: 18,837 listings — 51 per cent of the city's entire supply — are clustered in a single neighbourhood, the Centro Storico. Across Lazio, platform tourism more than doubled between 2018 and 2025, from 10.1 million to 21.1 million guest nights — the fastest growth among the six cities in this dossier. Italy introduced its first serious regulatory tool only in January 2025, with the national CIN registration code, alongside Rome's much-publicised ban on key boxes. But neither measure limits the number of nights a property can be rented, nor the number of homes that can be converted into short-term lets.

The rulebook · National ID code (CIN) mandatory since Jan 2025 · key-box ban in the centre · no cap on nights or licences.

No new licences have been issued since 2014, and the effect is visible: relative to its population, Barcelona now has the second-smallest short-let market of the six cities — but by far the most industrialised. Four listings in five are controlled by multi-property hosts, the highest share in this dossier, while a full quarter of the city's supply is concentrated in the famous Eixample grid.

The landmark Garcia-López study estimated that Airbnb increased rents by 1.9 per cent citywide and by 7 per cent in the neighbourhoods most exposed to the platform. Barcelona's response has been the most radical in Europe: all of its roughly 10,000 tourist licences are due to expire in November 2028, with no renewals. The plan was upheld by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2025.

The rulebook · Licence freeze since 2014 · PEUAT zoning since 2017 · all licences void by Nov 2028.

One Parisian dwelling in twenty is currently listed as an entire home on Airbnb — 69,265 apartments in one of Europe's densest housing markets. Yet Paris is also the capital of the amateur host: 89 per cent of listings are entire homes, but almost two-thirds of hosts have just one, typically renting out their own flat within the legal limit.

That limit has tightened steadily: registration became mandatory in 2017; the annual cap was set at 120 nights and then cut to 90 from January 2025 under the Le Meur law. Meanwhile, platform nights across Île-de-France more than doubled, from 14.4 million in 2018 to 30.2 million in 2025. Rents, helped in part by the reintroduction of rent controls, have risen less in Paris than in any of the other five cities in this dossier.

The rulebook · Registration since 2017 · 90-night cap on primary homes since Jan 2025 · fines up to €100k · rent control since 2019.

Berlin is the strongest case for regulation working. Its Zweckentfremdungsverbot — the ban on converting homes to other uses, applied to short-term lets since May 2016 — left a city of 3.8 million with fewer Airbnb listings than Lisbon, a city of just 567,000. Only 0.4 per cent of Berlin's dwellings are listed as entire homes, roughly one-twelfth the Paris rate, and the city says more than 8,000 apartments have been returned to the long-term rental market.

Berlin is also the only region in this dossier where platform tourism remains below its 2018 level. And yet long-term rents have risen by 85 per cent in a decade — the second sharpest climb of the six cities, after Lisbon. Airbnb was never Berlin's main housing problem. The deeper problem is a city gaining around 40,000 people a year while new construction has failed to keep pace.

The rulebook · Conversion ban (Zweckentfremdungsverbot) since 2014, enforced 2016 · permit + registration number required · 90-day cap on secondary homes · fines up to €500k.

Nowhere in this dossier have rents moved as dramatically as in Lisbon: the cost of a new lease roughly doubled between 2015 and 2025, the steepest increase in western Europe, while wages failed to keep pace. The pressure is most acute in the historic centre: Santa Maria Maior alone has 3,585 listings, clustered around the cathedral and the city's fado houses, while three-quarters of Lisbon's short-let supply is professionally managed.

Portugal's 2023 Mais Habitação law froze new short-term rental licences nationwide and, visibly in the data, brought the growth in platform nights almost to a halt. A new government partially reversed the measure a year later, returning much of the power over licences to municipalities. Lisbon, however, has kept its own freeze in the historic centre.

The rulebook · Containment zones since 2018 · national Alojamento Local licence freeze Oct 2023, devolved to municipalities late 2024 · Lisbon centre still frozen.

London has had a rule since 2015: entire homes may be let short-term for no more than 90 nights a year. But for years, there was essentially no way to know who was breaking it. There is still no registration scheme, leaving enforcement to borough officers who often lack the data to identify illegal lets.

The result is Europe's largest short-let market: 92,799 listings, including 10,921 in Westminster alone — a single borough holding almost as many listings as the whole of Berlin. After a decade of relatively flat rents, London prices jumped by more than a tenth in 2024 alone, the fastest annual increase on record. A national registration scheme for England has now been legislated for and is gradually being introduced.

The rulebook · 90-night annual cap since 2015 (Deregulation Act 2015, s.44) · no registration yet — national scheme in rollout · boroughs enforce case-by-case.

03 · The verdict

Did the crackdowns work?

The cleanest measure we have comes directly from Eurostat: since 2018, the agency has received guest-night data from the four major platforms, broken down by region. Indexing the figures to 2019 — the last full year before the pandemic — makes the policy story visible in the data.

Berlin 78

BANNED EARLY, ENFORCED HARD. The 2016 ban on converting homes to short-term lets predates our data — and that is precisely the point. Berlin enters the series with the market already heavily constrained, and remains the only region still below its 2018 level. Around 8,000 homes have been returned to the long-term rental market. Rents rose anyway.

Lisbon 112

FROZE, THEN BLINKED. After the October 2023 licence freeze, growth in the metropolitan area slowed to +8 per cent, while Rome's reached +24 per cent. The 2024 reversal returned licensing powers to city halls; Lisbon kept its historic centre frozen. It is the only clear mid-series inflection in the data.

Barcelona 131

THE LONG GOODBYE. A licence freeze since 2014 has capped the supply, but not the demand: platform nights are still a third higher than in 2019. The real experiment begins in November 2028, when every tourist licence in the city is due to expire at once — Europe's first full phase-out.

Rome 187

TOO LITTLE, TOO LATE. This is what weak regulation looks like: platform nights in Lazio have nearly doubled since 2019, the fastest growth among the five. Italy's CIN registration code, introduced in 2025, identifies hosts and properties — but does not limit how many homes can be offered or how many nights they can be rented.

Paris 202

CAPS WITHOUT A CEILING. Europe's strictest registration system and a 90-night annual cap have done little to restrain the overall market: platform nights have still doubled since 2019. The lesson is simple: caps constrain individual hosts, but they do not necessarily shrink the market when 77,000 listings can remain legal, one booking at a time.

London ?

NO DATA; NO RULE; NO REGISTER. The UK left the Eurostat platform-data agreement after Brexit, leaving London's 90-night rule to operate with remarkably little information about who is complying. England's national registration system is finally being rolled out — but for now, measurement is coming before enforcement.

04 · Methodology

Sources & methodology

Every number on this page is traceable to a public source. Where we built a series ourselves, we say so and link the underlying publisher.

Listings & maps
Inside Airbnb city snapshots of 15–30 June 2026, analysed by The Cormoran: counts, room types, host concentration, neighbourhood shares, and the coordinate grids and boundary basemaps behind every map here. Lisbon and Porto filtered to the municipality. Seven of Barcelona's 73 barrios — les Roquetes, la Trinitat Nova, Torre Baró, Ciutat Meridiana, Vallbona, la Trinitat Vella and Baró de Viver — are absent from that release; we redrew them from the Ajuntament de Barcelona's own boundary file so that the table under the map lists all 73. The release does not break out their listings, so they are named without a count, and the barrio column sums to 15,385 against a city total of 15,406: 21 listings sit in no neighbourhood row.
Snapshot files used: Rome · Barcelona · Paris · Berlin · Lisbon · London
How the city maps are framed
Each city map is framed on its own listings rather than on the municipal boundary: the frame is the extent of the dot grid. Neighbourhood boundaries that reach past it are cut at the edge — the industrial and park land at the rim of every one of these cities — and a few outlying areas fall outside the frame altogether. Barcelona is the case that matters: ten of its 73 barrios, at the top of Nou Barris and in Vallbona, sit above the frame and hold 20 listings between them, 0.13 per cent of the city. Framed or not, every area appears in the table beneath its map.
How the Europe map is built
Each of the 23 cities is marked at its true geographic coordinates — a pinpoint for the city itself, a ring around it for the size of its market. The measure is the rate: listings per 1,000 residents (total active listings ÷ population × 1,000). Ring area, not radius, is proportional to that rate (radius scales with its square root), because the eye reads area, so a city with twice the rate looks roughly twice as large, not four times. Every city is named, but no figures are printed on the map: names are placed by trying eight positions around each city and taking the first that clears the labels already placed; on a narrow screen the type grows to stay legible and the names that no longer fit are dropped. The table beneath the map carries the exact numbers for all 23 — listings, rate and entire-home share — and hovering any ring gives the same figures.
Platform nights
Eurostat experimental short-stay statistics, dataset tour_ce_omn12 — reported to Eurostat directly by Airbnb, Booking, Expedia and TripAdvisor. Retrieved August 2026. The UK is not covered.
How the index is calculated
The chart plots an index, not raw guest-nights. For each region, we divide every year's total by that region's own 2019 total, then ×100 — so 2019 always reads as 100, and every other year shows growth or decline relative to it. Example: Barcelona logged 20,717,244 guest-nights in 2019 and 27,181,807 in 2025; 27,181,807 ÷ 20,717,244 × 100 = 131, meaning 31% more nights than in 2019. Indexing each region to its own baseline is what lets very different market sizes (Paris vs. Berlin) share one axis. The underlying raw figures are in the table beneath the chart.
Housing stock
National censuses and dwelling-stock statistics, latest available: ISTAT, INE (Spain), INSEE, Amt für Statistik Berlin-Brandenburg, INE (Portugal), ONS. Shares of stock are approximations.
Rents
Indicative indices compiled by The Cormoran — they show direction and magnitude, not official statistics. Built from idealista (Rome), Incasòl (Barcelona), OLAP (Paris), IBB and ImmoScout24 (Berlin), INE and Confidencial Imobiliário (Lisbon), ONS Price Index of Private Rents (London).
How the "decade at a glance" panels work
The six small charts beside the opening text are the same rent indices as each city chapter, indexed to 2015 = 100, but drawn on one shared vertical scale rather than six independent ones — so a steeper line always means genuinely faster growth, never just a rescaled axis. The percentage on each card is the 2025 index value minus 100 (e.g. an index of 138 reads as "+38%"). Cards are ordered steepest first.
