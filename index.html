Airbnb and Europe's Rent Crisis

In 2024, travellers spent 854 million nights in Europe's platform rentals — a record. In the great cities, whole streets now sleep in someone else's bed. Six city portraits, three questions: how much of the housing is on Airbnb, what has it done to the rent, and did the crackdowns actually work?

August 2026 · Data: Inside Airbnb, Eurostat, national statistics · ~9 min read

Photo: Barcelona's Eixample — Logan Armstrong / Unsplash

There is a small metal box bolted to a drainpipe in every European city you have ever loved. Inside it is a key, and behind the key is an apartment that no one calls home. Rome has banned the boxes; Paris has capped the nights; Barcelona has promised to abolish the trade altogether. Yet in June 2026, the six cities in this dossier — Rome, Barcelona, Paris, Berlin, Lisbon and London — still counted more than a quarter of a million active listings on Airbnb between them.

The scale is no longer a niche phenomenon of the sharing economy. According to Eurostat, guests booked 854 million nights through Airbnb, Booking, Expedia and TripAdvisor in the EU in 2024 — up 19 per cent in a single year, and nearly a billion including the following season. Paris alone absorbed more platform nights than the whole of some member states. In the centre of Rome, one listing exists for every handful of households; in Lisbon's Santa Maria Maior, the medieval parish that gave the city its name and its fado, short lets outnumber many streets' remaining residents.

Does any of this raise the rent? The honest answer from the academic literature is: yes, measurably, though it is not the whole story. The most careful European study, on Barcelona, found that Airbnb activity pushed rents up 1.9 per cent citywide — and 7 per cent in the neighbourhoods where listings concentrate. In Berlin, researchers at DIW calculated that each additional entire-home listing displaces roughly a quarter to a third of a long-term rental unit. These are not catastrophic numbers on their own. But they land on top of a decade in which rents in these six cities rose between 18 and 100 per cent, and they concentrate precisely where housing is scarcest: the historic centre, the walkable district, the place people actually want to live.

So cities fought back, each with its own weapon. Berlin banned unpermitted entire-home lets as early as 2016. Paris built Europe's most sophisticated registration system and cut its annual cap to 90 nights. Lisbon froze new licences; Barcelona will let every one of its 10,000 licences expire in 2028; London has had a 90-night rule for a decade — with almost no way to enforce it; Rome, until last year, had barely any rules at all.

This dossier measures what happened next. The verdict, visible in the data: regulation can genuinely shrink the short-let market — Berlin is the proof — but nowhere has it, by itself, made the rent go down.

01 · The map

One continent, twenty-three markets

Airbnb pressure is not evenly spread. Port and heritage cities — Porto, Florence, Venice, Lisbon — carry three to four times the per-capita load of the northern capitals. Paris manages to be both: a metropolis with the intensity of a resort town.

Rome's market is a monoculture: 18,837 listings — 51 per cent of the entire city's supply — sit in a single neighbourhood, the Centro Storico. Platform tourism in Lazio more than doubled between 2018 and 2025, from 10.1 to 21.1 million guest nights, the fastest growth of our six cities. Italy only introduced its first serious instrument — the CIN national registration code — in January 2025, alongside Rome's celebrated ban on key-boxes. Neither caps the number of nights, nor the number of homes.

The rulebook · National ID code (CIN) mandatory since Jan 2025 · key-box ban in the centre · no cap on nights or licences.

No licence has been issued since 2014, and it shows: relative to its population, Barcelona now has the second-smallest market of the six — but by far the most industrial. Four listings in five belong to a multi-property host, the highest share in this dossier; a full quarter of the supply is packed into the Eixample grid. The landmark Garcia-López study put Airbnb's contribution at +1.9 per cent on rents citywide and +7 per cent in the most exposed neighbourhoods. The city's answer is the most radical in Europe: every one of its ~10,000 tourist licences expires, unrenewed, in November 2028 — a plan Spain's Constitutional Court upheld in 2025.

The rulebook · Licence freeze since 2014 · PEUAT zoning since 2017 · all licences void by Nov 2028.

One Parisian dwelling in twenty is currently listed as an entire home on Airbnb — 69,265 apartments, in the densest housing market on the continent. And yet Paris is also the capital of the amateur host: 89 per cent of listings are entire homes, but almost two-thirds of hosts have exactly one, renting their own flat within the legal cap. The cap itself keeps tightening — registration in 2017, 120 nights, then 90 from January 2025 under the Le Meur law — while platform nights in the Île-de-France kept doubling, from 14.4 million in 2018 to 30.2 million in 2025. Rents, held partly in check by re-imposed rent control, rose the least of our six cities.

The rulebook · Registration since 2017 · 90-night cap on primary homes since Jan 2025 · fines up to €100k · rent control since 2019.

Berlin is the control group. Its Zweckentfremdungsverbot — the ban on converting homes to other uses, enforced against short lets since May 2016 — left a city of 3.8 million with fewer listings than Lisbon, a city of 567,000. Just 0.4 per cent of Berlin's dwellings are entire-home listings, one-twelfth of the Paris rate, and the city says over 8,000 apartments have been returned to the long-term market. It is the only region in this dossier where platform tourism is still below its 2018 level. And yet asking rents rose 85 per cent in a decade — the sharpest climb of the six. Airbnb was never Berlin's main problem; a city growing by 40,000 people a year with a frozen construction pipeline is.

The rulebook · Conversion ban (Zweckentfremdungsverbot) since 2014, enforced 2016 · permit + registration number required · 90-day cap on secondary homes · fines up to €500k.

Nowhere in this dossier did rents move like Lisbon's: the price of a new lease roughly doubled between 2015 and 2025, the steepest climb in western Europe, while wages stayed Portuguese. The old town bears the weight — 3,585 listings in Santa Maria Maior alone, the parish of the cathedral and the fado houses — and three-quarters of the city's supply is professionally run. The 2023 Mais Habitação law froze new licences nationwide and, visibly in the data, slowed platform-night growth to a crawl; a new government partially reversed it a year later, handing the decision back to municipalities. Lisbon's own freeze, in the centre, stands.

The rulebook · Containment zones since 2018 · national Alojamento Local licence freeze Oct 2023, devolved to municipalities late 2024 · Lisbon centre still frozen.

London has had a rule since 2015 — entire homes may be let short-term for at most 90 nights a year — and essentially no way to know who breaks it: there is still no registration scheme, so enforcement falls to borough officers with no data. The result is the largest short-let market in Europe, 92,799 listings, with 10,921 in Westminster alone — more than all of Berlin. After a flat pandemic decade, London rents then jumped by more than a tenth in 2024 alone, the fastest on record. A national registration scheme for England has been legislated and is now, slowly, being switched on.

The rulebook · 90-night annual cap since 2015 (Deregulation Act 2015, s.44) · no registration yet — national scheme in rollout · boroughs enforce case-by-case.

03 · The verdict

Did the crackdowns work?

The cleanest measurement we have: Eurostat receives guest-night counts directly from the four big platforms, region by region, since 2018. Index them to 2019 — the last pre-pandemic year — and the policies write themselves onto the chart.

Berlin 77

BANNED EARLY, ENFORCED HARD. The 2016 conversion ban predates this data — which is the point: Berlin enters the series already suppressed and is the only region still below its 2018 level. Roughly 8,000 homes returned to the rental market. Rents rose anyway.

Lisbon 112

FROZE, THEN BLINKED. After the Oct 2023 licence freeze, growth in the metro area slowed to +8% while Rome ran at +24%. The 2024 reversal returned the power to city halls; Lisbon kept its centre frozen. The only visible mid-series bend in the data.

Barcelona 131

THE LONG GOODBYE. A licence freeze since 2014 capped the fleet but not the demand: nights are a third above 2019. The real experiment starts November 2028, when every licence in the city expires at once — Europe's first full phase-out.

Rome 187

THE COUNTERFACTUAL. This is what no meaningful regulation looks like: platform nights in Lazio nearly doubled against 2019, the fastest of the five. The CIN code (2025) identifies hosts; it does not limit them.

Paris 202

CAPS WITHOUT A CEILING. The strictest registration system in Europe, a 90-night cap — and platform nights still doubled since 2019. Caps discipline individual hosts; they do not cap a city where 77,000 listings each stay legal one booking at a time.

London —

NO DATA, NO RULE, NO REGISTER. The UK dropped out of the Eurostat platform agreement with Brexit — fittingly, since London's 90-night rule also runs on missing data. Registration for England is finally in rollout; measurement will precede enforcement.

04 · Method

Sources & method

Every number on this page is traceable to a public source. Where we built a series ourselves, we say so and link the underlying publisher.

Listings & maps
Inside Airbnb city snapshots of 15–30 June 2026, analysed by The Cormoran: counts, room types, host concentration, neighbourhood shares, and the coordinate grids and boundary basemaps behind every map here. Lisbon and Porto filtered to the municipality.
Snapshot files used: Rome · Barcelona · Paris · Berlin · Lisbon · London
Platform nights
Eurostat experimental short-stay statistics, dataset tour_ce_omn12 — reported to Eurostat directly by Airbnb, Booking, Expedia and TripAdvisor. Retrieved August 2026. The UK is not covered.
Housing stock
National censuses and dwelling-stock statistics, latest available: ISTAT, INE (Spain), INSEE, Amt für Statistik Berlin-Brandenburg, INE (Portugal), ONS. Shares of stock are approximations.
Rents
Indicative indices compiled by The Cormoran — they show direction and magnitude, not official statistics. Built from idealista (Rome), Incasòl (Barcelona), OLAP (Paris), IBB and ImmoScout24 (Berlin), INE and Confidencial Imobiliário (Lisbon), ONS Price Index of Private Rents (London).
Peer-reviewed research
